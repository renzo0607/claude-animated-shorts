## Facts (check before recording)
- 17 October 1814, ~5:30 pm, Meux & Co's Horse Shoe Brewery, corner of Tottenham Court Road / Oxford Street, London.
- Vat: ~22 ft (6.7 m) tall wooden vat of porter, ~610,000 L, held by massive iron hoops.
- About an hour earlier a ~700 lb (~320 kg) iron hoop slipped off. Storehouse clerk George Crick was told it was no
  cause for concern (it happened 2-3 times a year). He was writing a note to his superior about it when the vat burst.
- The burst ruptured neighbouring vats: ~1,470,000 L (323,000 imperial gallons) in total. It broke through the brewery's
  back wall (~25 ft high, bricks) into the St Giles rookery, one of London's poorest, most crowded slums.
- Wave reported ~15 ft (4.6 m). Flooded cellars, destroyed two houses; in a house on New Street a wake was being held
  for a 2-year-old boy, and five mourners died.
- Brewery workers were rescued from the rubble (one taken to hospital).
- 8 dead (women and children). Inquest verdict: death "by casualty / act of God"; nobody held responsible.
- The brewery was nearly ruined but Parliament refunded the excise duty paid on the lost beer (~£7,250).
- Afterwards the industry gradually replaced big wooden vats with lined concrete vats.
- AVOID the myth of crowds drinking from the gutters: contemporary reports don't support it.
Sources: Wikipedia "London Beer Flood"; The Times / Morning Chronicle reports Oct 1814 (as cited there);
Historic UK "The London Beer Flood". Re-check every number on recording day.

## Script (voice-over, read straight through)
In 1814, London was hit by a tidal wave, and it was made of beer.
It's October, and the Horse Shoe Brewery is the pride of the neighbourhood.
Its biggest vat is twenty-two feet tall, held together by iron hoops, and packed with half a million litres of dark porter.
One afternoon, a seven-hundred-pound hoop just slides off and crashes to the floor.
The clerk isn't worried. It happens a few times a year.
An hour later, he's writing a note to his boss about it, when the vat explodes.
It cracks open the vats next to it, and over a million litres of beer smash straight through the back wall.
A fifteen-foot wave of porter rolls into St Giles, one of the poorest corners of London.
It floods the cellars, flattens two houses, and pours into a room where a family is holding a wake.
Luckily, the workers inside the brewery were pulled out alive.
Unluckily, the beer had already left the building.
You see, a liquid pushes out on every wall that holds it, and the deeper it gets, the harder it pushes.
Ever stuffed a suitcase so full you had to sit on it? The straps are the only thing keeping it shut.
Those hoops were the straps. Lose one, and the rest carry the load, until the next one snaps.
Eight people died, and the court called it an act of God.
And the brewery? Parliament gave them back the tax they'd paid on all the beer they lost.
